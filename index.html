Live coverage — updated 29 August 2026

A glacier gave way above Langtang. The flood it unleashed reshaped three districts in an afternoon.

On 26 August, a rock-and-ice collapse off Langtang Lirung sent a wall of debris and meltwater nearly 100 km down the Lhende Khola, Bhote Koshi and Trishuli rivers, tearing through Rasuwa, Nuwakot and Dhading. This page tracks what is confirmed, what caused it, and where help is going.

The path of the flood

From a mountainside to a valley floor

Timeline
~08:00 local7,227 m · Langtang Lirung

A slope on the glacier's north face fails

A large section of rock and ice broke away from the northern flank of Langtang Lirung, just south of the Nepal–China border. The collapse released enormous energy — enough that it registered on seismometers around the world.

Minutes later~4,000 m · Lhende Khola

Rock, ice and meltwater become a debris flow

Friction from the falling rock generated heat, melting ice and mixing it with debris into a fast-moving slurry that poured into the Lhende Khola, gathering material as it descended the narrow valley.

Late morning~1,800 m · Timure & Syabrubesi, Rasuwa

The surge reaches settlements on the border

The flow entered the Bhote Koshi and struck Timure and Syabrubesi, sweeping away homes, vehicles, hydropower infrastructure and the highway linking Nepal to the Chinese border crossing at Gyirong.

Within the hourTrishuli River, downstream

Water rises roughly nine metres in thirty minutes

As the Bhote Koshi fed into the Trishuli, river levels downstream surged dramatically, catching villages and travellers along the Nuwakot and Dhading sections of the valley with little warning.

~3 hours laterUpper valley

A second slope failure follows the first

Researchers monitoring the event recorded a second, smaller landslide roughly three hours after the initial collapse — a reminder that slopes destabilised by the first failure remain hazardous.

Following daysRasuwa · Nuwakot · Dhading

Search, rescue and the count begins

Nepal Army, Police and Armed Police Force units, joined by Chinese rescue teams on the border side, have been clearing roads, searching debris fields and relocating stranded travellers as the toll continues to be confirmed.

What caused it

Not an earthquake — a warming glacier let go

Scientists who reviewed seismic and satellite data, including researchers cited by USGS and Columbia University, describe the event as a rock-ice avalanche and debris flow triggered by a rapid slope failure on the Langtang glacier, not a tectonic earthquake — despite a seismic signal large enough to be mistaken for one.

Studies of the Langtang glaciers have shown they are warming and retreating at an accelerating rate. Researchers point to that long-term thinning as the underlying condition that made this kind of sudden, catastrophic collapse possible.

The same mechanism — heat generated by a falling rock-ice mass melting into the debris it carries — has produced smaller but similar floods along the Nepal–China border in recent monsoon seasons, including events in July 2025 and July 2026 that damaged the same bridges and hydropower stations.

Dispatches

Missing persons · Cross-border

Hundreds of foreign travellers unaccounted for, including large tour groups

Among those still out of contact are dozens of American and Australian citizens, many travelling in group tours through Rasuwa when the flood hit. Families abroad have described frantic, unanswered calls to relatives who were last in touch from Kathmandu or the trekking route days earlier.

Response · China side

Rescue teams clear the Gyirong border road

On the Tibetan side of the border, state media report hundreds of metres of damaged road have been reopened near the Gyirong crossing, with firefighters, rescue dogs, vehicles and drones deployed to reach stranded villagers and tourists.

Humanitarian · Children

Aid agencies warn of risks facing displaced children

UNICEF and partner agencies are distributing hygiene kits, therapeutic food and mental-health support across Rasuwa, Nuwakot and Dhading, warning that children separated from families face heightened risk of malnutrition and exploitation in the aftermath.

Science

A pattern researchers have been warning about

Glaciologists studying the Langtang region have documented accelerating ice loss for years. This event, and smaller repeats of it in each of the last two monsoon seasons, are being cited as an early, severe example of the kind of glacial hazard a warming Himalaya is expected to produce more often.

How to help

Support

Support relief agencies

UNICEF, the Nepal Red Cross and other established humanitarian organisations are running active appeals for the Rasuwa–Nuwakot–Dhading response. Giving to a known agency working on the ground is generally more effective than sending goods directly.

Looking for someone?

Nepal's National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority and local police in Rasuwa, Nuwakot and Dhading are coordinating missing-person reports. Foreign nationals' families should also contact their country's embassy in Kathmandu.
